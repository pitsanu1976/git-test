Why Weibull?

Most failure data from rotating equipment, valves, instruments and electrical components does not follow a simple bell curve. The Weibull distribution, introduced by Waloddi Weibull in 1951, is flexible enough to model all three phases of the classic "bathtub" life: early-life failures, random failures and wear-out. Two parameters describe it:

β: shape parameter

Tells you how the item fails, meaning whether the failure rate is falling, constant or rising with age. This is the number that drives the maintenance decision.

η: scale parameter (characteristic life)

Tells you when. At t = η, 63.2% of the population has failed, whatever the value of β. It uses the same units as your data (hours, cycles, starts).

A third parameter, γ (location / failure-free life), is sometimes added when no failure can happen before a minimum age. Most maintenance work uses the 2-parameter form (γ = 0).

The key equations

Reliability: probability of surviving to time t

R(t) = exp[ −(t / η)β ]

Unreliability (CDF): probability of failing by t

F(t) = 1 − exp[ −(t / η)β ]

Probability density (PDF)

f(t) = (β/η)·(t/η)β−1·exp[ −(t/η)β ]

Hazard (instantaneous failure rate)

h(t) = (β/η)·(t/η)β−1

Mean time to failure

MTTF = η · Γ(1 + 1/β)

Bx life: age by which x% have failed

Bx = η · [ −ln(1 − x/100) ]1/β

Linearised form for plotting. Taking logs twice gives a straight line:

ln[ −ln(1 − F) ] = β·ln(t) − β·ln(η)

Plot ln(t) on x and ln[−ln(1−F)] on y. The slope is β, and η is the time where the line crosses F = 63.2%.

Reading β: the bathtub curve and what to do about it

The hazard function h(t) shows how the failure rate changes with age. The chart compares three typical shapes with η = 1,000 h.

β rangeFailure patternTypical causesMaintenance implication
β < 1 Infant mortality: failure rate decreases with age Poor installation, manufacturing defects, wrong assembly, inadequate commissioning, maintenance-induced errors Do not do time-based overhaul; it resets the clock to the riskiest period. Improve QA/QC, burn-in, installation procedures and precision maintenance.
β ≈ 1 Random: constant failure rate (exponential) External events, overloads, operator error, mixed failure modes, many electronics Age-based replacement adds no value. Use condition monitoring (if a P-F interval exists), failure-finding tests for hidden functions, redundancy, or run-to-failure.
1 < β ≤ 4 Early wear-out: failure rate rises Bearing fatigue, corrosion, erosion, low-cycle fatigue Scheduled replacement / overhaul can be cost-effective, especially when β > 2. Combine with CBM where possible.
β > 4 Rapid wear-out: failures cluster tightly around η Material ageing, erosion of seals, belts, brake pads, stress corrosion Highly predictable. Fixed-interval replacement before the B1–B10 life is usually best.

Rule of thumb. Time-based preventive maintenance only pays off when β > 1 and the cost of an unplanned failure is clearly higher than a planned replacement (Cf / Cp > 1). If either is false, look at condition-based or design-out options.

Optimal preventive replacement interval

For wear-out modes (β > 1), the age-replacement model finds the replacement age T that minimises long-run cost per operating hour:

C(T) = [ Cp·R(T) + Cf·F(T) ] ÷ ∫0T R(t) dt

Cp is the cost of a planned replacement. Cf is the total cost of an unplanned failure (repair, lost production, secondary damage, HSE exposure).

A practical Weibull workflow for maintenance teams

  1. Define the failure mode clearly. Analyse one mode at a time (e.g. "mechanical seal leak", not "pump failure"). Mixing modes distorts β, often pulling it toward 1.
  2. Collect clean age data from the CMMS. Record operating hours or cycles since the last renewal, not calendar dates. Include suspensions: units still running, or removed for other reasons.
  3. Plot and fit. Use median-rank regression for a quick look and MLE for decisions. Check R² and look for a curved or "dog-leg" plot, which points to mixed modes or a failure-free period (γ).
  4. Interpret β. β < 1 means fix quality and installation. β ≈ 1 means CBM or failure-finding. β > 1 means consider time-based replacement.
  5. Quantify consequences. Estimate Cp and Cf, including production loss and safety/environmental impact.
  6. Set the strategy and interval. Use the cost-optimal T*, the Bx life for safety-critical items, or the P-F interval for CBM tasks.
  7. Feed back and update. Re-run the analysis as new failures and suspensions arrive, and track MTBF and bad-actor trends.

Common pitfalls

Ignoring suspensions

Leaving out running units makes life look shorter than it really is, which leads to over-maintenance.

Mixing failure modes

A blend of infant mortality and wear-out can look like β ≈ 1 and hide both problems.

Too few data points

With n < 5, β is very uncertain. Use Weibayes with a known β from industry data such as OREDA.

Calendar vs operating time

Use run-hours or cycles. Standby time distorts the ages unless the mode is time-driven, such as corrosion.
